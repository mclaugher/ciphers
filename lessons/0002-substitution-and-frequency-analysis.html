Substitution & Frequency Analysis

Caesar fell because it had only 25 keys. So we build a cipher with four hundred septillion keys instead — and watch it fall anyway, to a ninth-century idea you can use with a pencil.

Where we left off. In Lesson 01 you cracked a Caesar cipher by trying all 25 shifts. The lesson there was Kerckhoffs's principle: judge a cipher by its keys. The obvious fix for Caesar is to give it more keys. This lesson follows that fix to its limit — and finds the limit isn't where you'd expect.

1 · From a shift to a full scramble

A Caesar cipher slides the whole alphabet by one amount, so the cipher alphabet keeps its order. A monoalphabetic substitution cipher drops that rule: each letter may map to any letter, in any order — as long as the mapping is one-to-one.1 The key is no longer a single number; it's the entire scrambled alphabet.

2 · The keyspace explodes

How many keys is that? The first plaintext letter can map to any of 26 ciphertext letters, the second to any of the 25 left, the third to any of the remaining 24, and so on. Multiply them: 26 × 25 × 24 × … × 1, written 26! ("26 factorial").1 That comes to:

403,291,461,126,605,635,584,000,000

Four hundred septillion keys — about 4 × 1026.1 Caesar's brute-force attack is now hopeless: an attacker checking a million keys every second would still need over ten trillion years to try them all — far longer than the age of the universe.2

Key idea A huge keyspace is necessary for security, but it is not sufficient. Brute force is only one way in. A cipher with more keys than there are atoms in your body can still leak its secret through a side door — if its output carries a pattern.

3 · The side door: language has a fingerprint

Here is the flaw. In a substitution cipher, one plaintext letter always becomes the same ciphertext letter. So whatever is special about a letter in English travels intact into the ciphertext. And the letter E is very special: it is the most common letter in English, about 12.7% of all text. T, A, O, I, N follow.3

So the most common letter in the ciphertext is probably E in disguise. Count the letters, line up the counts against English, and the key starts to dissolve — no matter how many keys there were. This is frequency analysis, first written down by the Arab polymath al-Kindi in Baghdad around 850 CE, in the oldest known book on codebreaking.4 It is the single most important idea in the history of cryptanalysis.

Kerckhoffs, sharpened Counting keys is not enough. You must also ask: does the ciphertext betray the structure of the plaintext? Substitution scrambles the alphabet but preserves letter frequencies — so it answers "yes," and that is its undoing.
