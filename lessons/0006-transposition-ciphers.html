Transposition Ciphers

Every cipher you've met so far replaces letters. This whole family does the opposite: it keeps every letter exactly as it is — and hides the message in the order.

A fork in the road. Caesar, substitution, Vigenère, the one-time pad — all belong to one family: substitution, where each letter is swapped for another. There is a second, equally ancient family: transposition, where the letters stay but get rearranged. The Spartans used one around the 7th century BCE — the scytale, a staff you wound a strip of leather around to read the message.1

1 · The rail fence — writing in a zigzag

The simplest transposition writes the message in a zigzag across a few "rails," then reads it off one rail at a time. Same letters, new order.2

2 · Columnar transposition — the keyword version

The serious by-hand transposition writes the message into a grid row by row under a keyword, then reads the columns off in the order the keyword's letters fall alphabetically. The keyword is the key.2

Columnar transposition

Columns are numbered by their keyword letter's place in the alphabet; read them in that order (1, 2, 3 …). The final row is padded with X.

3 · The dead giveaway — and the crack

Transposition has a glaring tell. Because it never changes a single letter, the letter frequencies are untouched: if the plaintext had fourteen E's, so does the ciphertext.2 Run frequency analysis and you'll see a perfectly normal English profile — which tells you instantly it's a transposition, not a substitution. You then break it by anagramming: rearranging the letters back into sense by trying the possible geometries.

4 · Why this matters more than it looks

On its own, transposition is weak — the frequencies betray it. But something powerful happens when you combine the two families. Substitution hides which letters you used; transposition hides where they were. Claude Shannon named these two jobs confusion and diffusion, and argued a strong cipher needs both.3

Confusion & diffusion — the shape of modern ciphers Modern block ciphers like AES are built as a substitution–permutation network: they alternate substitution steps (confusion) with transposition steps (diffusion), over and over, on bits instead of letters.3 Every cipher in this whole course has been one of these two moves — modern cryptography just braids them together, many times, very fast.
