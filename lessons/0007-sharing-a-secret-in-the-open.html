Sharing a Secret in the Open

Every cipher in this course has had the same Achilles' heel: you must already share a key. In 1976, two mathematicians showed how two strangers can agree on a secret key while an enemy records every word they say — and still be safe.

The 2,000-year-old bottleneck. The one-time pad is unbreakable, yet barely used, because you must somehow deliver the key in advance (Lesson 05). Caesar, Vigenère, all of them — same problem. For all of history the answer was physical: meet in person, or trust a courier. If you could intercept the key, the cipher didn't matter. This is the key-distribution problem, and it shackled cryptography until almost yesterday.

1 · The trick, in paint

Here's the idea that broke the bottleneck, with no maths at all. Mixing paint is easy; un-mixing it — separating a blend back into its colours — is practically impossible. That one-way-ness is the whole secret.1

The eavesdropper saw the common paint and both public mixes — but to find the shared colour she'd have to un-mix a secret back out. She can't.

2 · The same trick, in numbers (Diffie–Hellman)

In 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman (building on an idea of Ralph Merkle) turned that paint into arithmetic.1 The one-way function is modular exponentiation: raising a number to a power and keeping the remainder (ga mod p) is quick, but working backwards from the result to the exponent — the discrete logarithm — has no known fast method.2

…and to get the secret she'd need a from A = ga mod p — the discrete log. No fast way is known.
One-way functions — the engine of modern cryptography The breakthrough isn't hiding the secret better; it's building on a calculation that's easy forwards and infeasible backwards. Security no longer rests on a pre-shared key, but on a hard maths problem. You may publish everything about the method and the public numbers — Kerckhoffs would approve — and the secret still holds.

3 · Why this changed the world

Diffie and Hellman had split the key in two: something public you can shout across the room, and something private you never share. That idea — public-key cryptography — is what lets your browser agree on a key with a website it has never met, over a network full of strangers. The padlock in your address bar is this lesson, running billions of times a second.2

One year later, in 1977, three researchers turned the same one-way idea into a full encryption system — RSA — where anyone can lock a message with your public key, but only your private key can open it. That's the next lesson.
