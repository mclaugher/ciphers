The Caesar Cipher

Where every cipher begins. By the end of this page you'll encrypt, decrypt, and crack a Caesar cipher with nothing but your head — and you'll have met the one idea that all of cryptography is built on.

Why this, first? Your mission is to understand how ciphers work. The Caesar cipher is the simplest one that's still a real cipher, so it lets us name the moving parts — plaintext, key, ciphertext — that every later cipher reuses. Master this and the rest of the journey is variations on a theme.

1 · The idea

A Caesar cipher replaces each letter of your message with the letter a fixed number of places further along the alphabet.1 That fixed number is the whole secret. Julius Caesar himself used a shift of 3 — turning A into D — to protect military messages, as recorded by the Roman historian Suetonius.1

Three words we'll use for the rest of the journey:

  • Plaintext — the readable message, e.g. ATTACK AT DAWN.
  • Key — the secret. For Caesar, just the shift number (1–25).
  • Ciphertext — the scrambled output you'd actually send.
Key idea The method (shift the letters) and the key (shift by how much) are two different things. Anyone may know you're using a Caesar cipher — but without the key, they still have to do some work. Hold on to that distinction; in §3 it becomes the most important idea in cryptography.

3 · The one idea behind all cryptography

Here's the question that separates a toy from a tool: what has to stay secret? In 1883 Auguste Kerckhoffs argued that a cipher should stay secure even if the enemy knows everything about it except the key.2 Claude Shannon later put it bluntly: "the enemy knows the system."2

So you never bet your secret on hiding the method — methods leak. You bet it on the key. This is why we keep asking, for every cipher: how many keys are there, and can an attacker just try them all?

Kerckhoffs's principle A cipher's security must rest on the secrecy of the key alone — not on hiding how the cipher works. Judge every cipher by its keys, never by its mystery.

4 · So how strong is Caesar? Crack one.

Apply the test. A Caesar key is a shift from 1 to 25 — so there are only 25 possible keys.1 An attacker doesn't need to be clever; they can write out all 25 shifts and look for the one that reads as English. That's a brute-force attack, and against Caesar it takes about a minute by hand.
