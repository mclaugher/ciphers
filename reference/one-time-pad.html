The One-Time Pad

The only cipher proven unbreakable — Vigenère with a key that is random, as long as the message, and never reused. Perfect secrecy, at the price of an enormous key.

The method (letter version)

encrypt: C = (P + K) mod 26 decrypt: P = (C − K) mod 26

…where K is the next letter of the random pad, used once. It's exactly the Vigenère rule — the difference is entirely in the key. (See A = 0 … Z = 25.)

Worked example.

message H E L L O
pad X M C K Q (random, used once)
cipher E Q N V E

H(7)+X(23)=30→4=E, E(4)+M(12)=16=Q, … Lose the pad and even the sender can't read it.

The four rules — break one and it's broken

  • Random. Every key letter truly random — never a word, phrase, or pattern.
  • Long. The key is at least as long as the message.
  • Once. Each stretch of key is used a single time, then destroyed.
  • Secret & pre-shared. Both parties hold the same pad, exchanged securely in advance.

Why it's perfectly secret

For any ciphertext, every plaintext of that length is reachable with some key — and all keys are equally likely. So the ciphertext gives an attacker no information at all, even with unlimited computing power. Claude Shannon proved this in 1949, and proved that perfect secrecy requires a key at least as long as the message.1

The traps

Reuse = a two-time pad.

If one pad encrypts two messages, subtracting the ciphertexts cancels the key: C₁ − C₂ = P₁ − P₂. The key vanishes and the messages leak — this broke reused Soviet pads in the VENONA project.2

The key-distribution problem.

To share one secret message you must first secretly share a key just as big. The secret-sharing problem isn't solved — it's merely moved. That practical wall is why modern cryptography exists.
