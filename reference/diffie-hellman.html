Diffie–Hellman Key Exchange

How two people who have never met agree on a shared secret over a tapped line — publishing everything except their two private numbers. (Diffie, Hellman & Merkle, 1976.)

The one-way function

forward (easy): A = ga mod p backward (hard): given A, g, p — find a ← the "discrete logarithm"

Easy to compute, infeasible to reverse for large p. That asymmetry is the whole security. (Mixing paint is easy; un-mixing is not.)

The exchange

StepAliceBob
Agree in publica prime p and base g — anyone may know these
Pick a private secretab
Send (in the open)A = ga mod pB = gb mod p
Compute shared secrets = Ba mod ps = Ab mod p

Both land on the same number because (gb)a = gab = (ga)b (mod p). The shared secret gab mod p is never transmitted.

Worked example (tiny numbers)

public: p = 23, g = 5 Alice: a = 6 → A = 56 mod 23 = 8 Bob: b = 15 → B = 515 mod 23 = 19 Alice: s = 196 mod 23 = 2 Bob: s = 815 mod 23 = 2 ✓ same secret

Real Diffie–Hellman uses primes hundreds of digits long, so the discrete-log shortcut is hopeless. The eavesdropper sees p, g, A, B — never a, b, or s.

Why it mattered

It split the key into a public half and a private half — the birth of public-key cryptography. The same handshake secures https:// today. One year later, RSA extended the idea from key-exchange to full encryption.
